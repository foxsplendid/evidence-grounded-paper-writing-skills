# Framing and outline

## Frame a question before proposing a contribution

1. Name the object of study, comparison, and inference the reader should be able to make. For an empirical paper, identify which observation would change the answer. For a review, identify the comparison or unresolved question the reader cannot answer from individual articles alone.
2. Map the closest alternatives or prior work. Compare what was measured, the setting and comparator, and the reach of the evidence. Scope novelty to the literature actually examined and identify missing comparators when the search is incomplete.
3. State the central answer at the strength warranted by current evidence. With preliminary results, give a provisional answer or an open question. For each major claim, note its supporting material and which alternative explanation or uncertainty matters for the interpretation.

## Diagnose an existing manuscript

Make a reverse outline using the actual claim or function of each substantive paragraph. Locate it by section and opening words, or another stable anchor. Record what the paragraph contributes and what its evidence allows the reader to infer. An entry can indicate a passage that provides context or supplies a transition without making an independent claim.

Read the outline as a reader encountering the argument for the first time. Look for evidence arriving after the claim it is needed to interpret, examples cycling through the same point, a paragraph answering a different question, and transitions that only announce a change of topic. Identify which apparently redundant examples supply independent confirmation, conflicting results, or a different condition worth retaining.

For each consequential change, show the old sequence, proposed sequence, and practical benefit. If alternatives would change the article's emphasis, explain what each makes easier to demonstrate and what evidence it requires. Link any useful table or figure to the comparison it would let the reader make.

## Prepare a plan that an author can assess

Use a simple mapping when it helps.

| Section or paragraph | Question or claim | Evidence and its reach | Connection to the next point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

Include a short scaffold for each reorganized section, an evidence gap requiring search or verification, and a representative before/after example if requested. If a substantial cut leaves the claim thin, restore an informative comparison, a conflicting result, or an explicit evidential step. Decide which discussion can be shorter by checking whether its removal changes the scientific inference.

The plan is ready when the section sequence answers the central question, each proposed claim has a source or a visible gap, and the author can see which passages would move, merge, or be added.
